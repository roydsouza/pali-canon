## Overview

Chapters IV through VII form the *subject compendium* of the Visuddhimagga's samādhi book. Chapter III named the 40 meditation subjects; these four chapters give detailed practical instruction for the first 26: the ten kasiṇas (Ch. IV–V), ten foulness meditations (Ch. VI), and ten recollections (Ch. VII). Each chapter follows the same arc: object construction → development of the learning sign → counterpart sign → access concentration → absorption.

Chapter VIII (ānāpānasati) and Chapter IX (brahmavihārās) receive their own chapters because of their breadth and importance; they are treated in separate vault files.

## Chapter IV — Earth Kasiṇa (*Paṭhavīkasiṇakathā*)

### §§ 1–85 — Constructing the kasiṇa disk

The **earth kasiṇa** is treated at greatest length because Buddhaghosa uses it to establish the procedural template for all kasiṇa practice. The same sequence — building the device, developing the learning sign, the counterpart sign, and entering jhāna — governs all ten kasiṇas with only the object changing.

**Making the earth disk**: a circular frame, about a handspan in diameter, is filled with clay of a uniform, "dawn-colour" (*uggaha-nimitta*) brown. The frame can be portable (a cloth spread over a tray of clay) or fixed (a disk plastered on the wall or floor). The surface should be smooth, free from pebbles, root-fragments, and colour variation. This uniformity matters: the mind will replicate whatever it sees, and irregularities in the disk become irregularities in the mental sign.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on constructing the kasiṇa disk (§§ 1–30)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

### The learning sign (*uggaha-nimitta*) and the counterpart sign (*paṭibhāga-nimitta*)

With the disk prepared, the meditator sits at a comfortable distance, gazes at it with "medium energy" — not straining, not sluggish — and attends to it as **earth, earth**. The eyes may be open or half-open; closing them entirely risks losing the visual object before the mental image is stable.

Two mental images develop through practice:

| Sign | Pali | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Learning sign** | *Uggaha-nimitta* | The mental replica of the disk as it appeared: same texture, same imperfections, same color variations. Arises when the meditator can hold the object mentally with eyes closed. |
| **Counterpart sign** | *Paṭibhāga-nimitta* | A purified, luminous version of the disk — flawless, not limited by the physical disk's properties. Arises when neighbourhood concentration has been reached. |

The counterpart sign is not constructed; it arises by itself as the mind's concentration purifies the image. Once it appears, the meditator should not try to examine it analytically. Instead: *stay with it, enter into it.* The same transition from the counterpart sign into absorption governs all kasiṇa and ānāpānasati practice.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on the two nimittas (§§ 31–65)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

### Jhāna development on the earth kasiṇa

Once the counterpart sign is stable, the meditator develops it through the five jhāna factors: the mind *places* itself on the sign (vitakka), *stays* on the sign (vicāra), is *suffused with joy* (pīti) as the hindrances recede, *rests in happiness* (sukha), and becomes *unified* around the sign (ekaggatā). The progression through the four jhānas follows the standard schema described in [[pakarana/visuddhimagga/vsm11_samadhi|Chapter XI]].

## Chapter V — The Remaining Nine Kasiṇas (*Sesadasakasiṇakathā*)

### §§ 1–30 — Water, fire, wind

The procedure is identical to the earth kasiṇa; only the object changes.

- **Water kasiṇa** (*āpo-kasiṇa*): a bowl of clear, still water without reflections of the face; or a naturally still pool. Attend to it as *water, water*.
- **Fire kasiṇa** (*tejo-kasiṇa*): a hole in a mat or wall through which a fire is visible. The meditator sees the fire through the hole, attending to the *colour and shape* of the flame, not to its burning. Attend as *fire, fire*.
- **Wind kasiṇa** (*vāyo-kasiṇa*): attending to wind striking the skin, or to a visible movement (leaves, grass) that directly signals wind. More subtle than the other elements; the learning sign is harder to stabilize.

### §§ 31–100 — Color kasiṇas and the space and light kasiṇas

The four **color kasiṇas** (blue, yellow, red, white) are circles of pure colour — a cloth, a plate, or flower petals arranged in a disk of the appropriate colour. These are particularly recommended by Buddhaghosa for the *dosa* (hateful/aversive) temperament, since dwelling on colour generates pleasure rather than revulsion.

- **Blue** (*nīla*): dark blue, the colour of a full-blown blue lotus
- **Yellow** (*pīta*): the colour of a golden champak flower
- **Red** (*lohita*): the colour of a red hibiscus
- **White** (*odāta*): the colour of the full moon or a white jasmine flower

The **light kasiṇa** (*āloka-kasiṇa*) uses a circle of light — sunlight or lamplight passing through a hole in a wall, projected as a luminous disk on the floor. It is described as particularly suited to developing the divine eye (clairvoyance), a supernormal power discussed in Chapter XII.

The **space kasiṇa** (*ākāsa-kasiṇa*) uses the space within a hole in a wall, bounded by its edges. The meditator attends to the *bounded space*, not the wall. This kasiṇa is a conceptual object and is somewhat more difficult for beginners.

## Chapter VI — Foulness Contemplations (*Asubhakathā*)

### §§ 1–110 — The ten corpse stages

The ten *asubha* (foulness) meditation objects are the stages of decomposition of a corpse, observed directly — in Buddhaghosa's day, in charnel grounds open to the sky. The canonical source for this practice is [[mn119|MN 119]] (kāyagatāsati) and the *kāyānupassanā* section of [[mn10|MN 10]] (Satipaṭṭhānasutta).

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Bloated (*uddhumātaka*) | 1–3 days after death; swollen, discolored, malodorous |
| 2. Livid (*vinīlaka*) | Blue-red mottled coloring |
| 3. Festering (*vipubbaka*) | Oozing matter, broken skin |
| 4. Cut-up (*vicchiddaka*) | Severed by weapons or animals |
| 5. Gnawed (*vikkhāyitaka*) | Partially eaten by animals |
| 6. Scattered (*vikkhittaka*) | Bones and parts scattered in different directions |
| 7. Hacked-and-scattered (*hatavikkhittaka*) | Scattered after being hacked apart |
| 8. Bleeding (*lohitaka*) | Still fresh, blood visible |
| 9. Worm-infested (*puḷavaka*) | Permeated with worms |
| 10. Skeleton (*aṭṭhika*) | Bones only; may be further analysed into the 32 body parts |

**Purpose**: to counter the mind's habitual beautification of the body — the tendency to see the physical form as intrinsically attractive. The foulness meditations specifically counter *rāga* (lust/attachment), which is why Buddhaghosa assigns them to the rāga temperament.

**Jhāna range**: foulness meditations support only the *first jhāna*. The object — a repulsive corpse — requires *vitakka* and *vicāra* to stay in contact with it; without applied and sustained thought, the mind slips away. There is no deeper absorption on these objects.

**Modern application**: most contemporary practitioners access these meditations through the 32 body-parts contemplation embedded in kāyagatāsati ([[mn119|MN 119]]) rather than through direct corpse observation.

## Chapter VII — The Recollections (*Cha-Anussatikathā*)

### §§ 1–180 — The six primary recollections

Chapter VII covers all ten recollections. The first six — *Buddhānussati, Dhammānussati, Saṅghānussati, Sīlānussati, Cāgānussati, Devatānussati* — have a direct canonical source in [[an6_10|AN 6.10]] and are the practices most commonly recommended to lay practitioners.

| Recollection | Object | Practice instruction |
|---|---|---|
| **Buddhānussati** | Qualities of the Buddha (the 9 epithets) | Recite and attend to each quality: *itipi so bhagavā arahaṃ sammāsambuddho...* |
| **Dhammānussati** | Qualities of the Dhamma (the 6 epithets) | *svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo...* |
| **Saṅghānussati** | Qualities of the Saṅgha (the 9 epithets) | *suppaṭipanno bhagavato sāvakasaṅgho...* |
| **Sīlānussati** | One's own virtue — recalling it as unbroken, untorn | Joy arises from the fact of one's own virtue; not pride but confidence |
| **Cāgānussati** | One's own generosity | Recall specific acts of giving; joy and lightness arise |
| **Devatānussati** | Qualities that caused devas to be reborn as devas | Attend to the same qualities (conviction, virtue, learning, generosity, wisdom) in oneself |

**Jhāna range of the six recollections**: access concentration only. The conceptual content is too rich and varied for the mind to achieve the unified stillness of full absorption. What arises instead is a luminous, joyful, protected quality of mind — *pīti* and *sukha* suffuse the mind without reaching ekaggatā. This is fully appropriate for daily life practice.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on the six recollections (§§ 1–100)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

### §§ 181–270 — Four additional recollections

**Maraṇasati** (mindfulness of death) — the canonical source is [[an6_19|AN 6.19]] and [[an6_20|AN 6.20]]. Buddhaghosa gives eight ways of developing this recollection: recalling that death will certainly come; examining corpses; recalling that great and powerful beings have died; examining the body's constant dependence on breath. Jhāna range: access concentration.

**Kāyagatāsati** (mindfulness of the body, 32 parts) — the canonical source is [[mn119|MN 119]]. Attending to the 32 body parts generates *asubha*-type concentration; the earth-kasiṇa element embedded in the bones and teeth can be used to develop up to the fourth jhāna. Jhāna range: up to the fourth jhāna (via the earth-kasiṇa embedded in the contemplation).

**Ānāpānasati** (mindfulness of breathing) — treated fully in [[pakarana/visuddhimagga/vsm08_anapanasati|Chapter VIII]]. Buddhaghosa gives only a summary here. Jhāna range: all four jhānas.

**Upasamānussati** (recollection of peace/nibbāna) — attending to nibbāna itself as the complete cessation of suffering. Buddhaghosa notes this is the most difficult recollection; it requires the meditator to turn toward what is, by its nature, beyond conceptual designation. Jhāna range: access concentration.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on maraṇasati and upasamānussati (§§ 181–270)

## Key Pali terms in this chapter

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **kasiṇa** | A totality device — a physical or natural object used as the initial basis for concentration |
| **uggaha-nimitta** | Learning sign; the mental image of the kasiṇa as it appeared to the senses, with its imperfections |
| **paṭibhāga-nimitta** | Counterpart sign; the purified, luminous mental image that arises at the threshold of access concentration |
| **asubha** | Foulness; unattractive; the ten contemplations of the corpse stages |
| **anussati** | Recollection; the ten objects of mindful calling-to-mind |
| **maraṇasati** | Mindfulness of death; one of the recollections |
| **kāyagatāsati** | Mindfulness of the body; the 32-parts contemplation |
| **upasamānussati** | Recollection of peace; direct turning of the mind toward nibbāna |
| **pīti** | Rapture; the quality of joyful well-being that arises in the recollections at access concentration |
