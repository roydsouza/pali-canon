## Overview

Chapter IX treats the four *brahmavihārās* (divine abidings or sublime attitudes) — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — in their role as **samādhi objects leading to jhāna**. This chapter's concern is not the ethical dimension (which the canonical suttas treat fully) but the meditative mechanics: how to develop each quality systematically, how to direct it in eleven ways toward specific categories of beings, and why three of the four produce only first-through-third jhāna while the fourth produces the fourth jhāna.

The brahmavihārās are unique among the 40 subjects in that they are **boundless** (*appamāṇa*) by nature — they cannot be limited to a single object. This unboundedness is both their distinctive quality and the explanation for their jhāna range.

## Part 1 — Loving-Kindness (*Mettā*)

### §§ 1–76 — Beginning with oneself

Buddhaghosa's first and most important instruction: **do not begin with a disliked person, a neutral person, or a highly beloved person.** Begin with *yourself*. The practitioner first develops the wish *May I be happy; may I be free from suffering* — not as a prayer but as an active mental state of goodwill directed at one's own existence.

Why begin with oneself? The canonical response (AN 4.99, MN 10) implies that one who cannot feel genuine goodwill toward oneself cannot sustain it toward others. Buddhaghosa is more precise: beginning with the loved person risks generating attachment (*pema*) rather than mettā; beginning with a disliked person generates friction; beginning with a neutral person generates neither warmth nor friction — the practice is unstable. Beginning with oneself generates the model of uncomplicated well-wishing that can then be extended.

### §§ 40–76 — The eleven categories

Once mettā has been stabilized toward oneself, it is extended outward in **eleven systematic categories**:

| Category | Pali | Description |
|---|---|---|
| All beings | *sabbe sattā* | Without exception |
| All breathing things | *sabbe pāṇā* | Beings with breath |
| All creatures | *sabbe bhūtā* | All who have come into being |
| All individuals | *sabbe puggalā* | All persons, named and unnamed |
| All with personality | *sabbe attabhāvapariyāpannā* | All embodied beings |
| All females | *sabbā itthiyo* | — |
| All males | *sabbe purisā* | — |
| All noble ones | *sabbe ariyā* | Stream-enterers through arahants |
| All non-noble ones | *sabbe anariyā* | Those not yet on the path |
| All humans | *sabbe manussā* | — |
| All non-humans | *sabbe amanussā* | Devas, petas, animals, and others |

Having extended mettā through the five unbounded categories, the meditator then extends it in the **ten directions**: east, west, north, south, northeast, northwest, southeast, southwest, above, below. Each of the eleven categories is radiated in each direction, giving 110 + 11 = **121 pervasions** in the full systematic practice. This unbounded directionality is the basis for the term *appamāṇa-mettā* — "immeasurable loving-kindness."

### Jhāna range of mettā

Mettā supports **first, second, and third jhāna**. It does not produce the fourth jhāna. Why? Because mettā is associated with *sukha* — the wish that beings be happy, and the mind's own happiness in wishing this. *Sukha* is the defining quality of the third jhāna and is transcended in the fourth. A mettā-mind cannot release the quality of warmth and pleasure that gives it its character; this is not a limitation but a structural feature of the quality.

## Part 2 — Compassion (*Karuṇā*)

### §§ 77–114 — Karuṇā: wishing beings free from suffering

**Compassion** arises by attending to beings who are suffering — oppressed, in pain, afflicted. The specific instruction: begin with a clearly suffering person (a sick person, a person in grief), generate the wish *may this person be free from suffering*, and let the quality of compassion — a willingness to take on another's suffering — arise.

As with mettā, one begins with a specific person and extends systematically. One avoids beginning with a dead person (the mind produces no warmth), a beloved person (generates grief rather than compassion), and an enemy (generates aversion). The progression: clearly suffering person → beloved person → neutral person → hostile person → all beings.

**Near and far enemies**: Buddhaghosa identifies the characteristic enemies of each brahmavihāra:
- *Near enemy of karuṇā*: worldly grief (*domanassa*) — which resembles compassion but is distress rather than the open quality of care
- *Far enemy of karuṇā*: cruelty (*vihiṃsā*)

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on developing karuṇā (§§ 77–100)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

**Jhāna range**: same as mettā — first through third jhāna. Karuṇā is associated with sukha (the happiness in caring), so the fourth jhāna is unavailable.

## Part 3 — Sympathetic Joy (*Muditā*)

### §§ 115–139 — Muditā: rejoicing in beings' happiness

**Sympathetic joy** is the delight in beings' prosperity, happiness, and success. It directly counters *aratī* (discontentment, envy). Buddhaghosa's instruction: begin with a genuinely happy person — a friend who is thriving, joyful, whose happiness is evident — and generate *sādhu sādhu* (bravo, well done) or *may this person's happiness not diminish*.

The near enemy of muditā is *pahāsa* — exuberance or mirth — which resembles sympathetic joy but is superficial excitement rather than genuine rejoicing. The far enemy is *arati* (aversion, boredom).

Muditā is the most difficult of the four brahmavihāras to develop in a world where suffering is prominent. Buddhaghosa acknowledges this: in a world of pain it is easier to feel compassion than joy. The answer is to start with people who are clearly happy and let the mind learn to find its footing in gladness before extending it to those in more ambiguous circumstances.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on developing muditā (§§ 115–139)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

**Jhāna range**: same as mettā and karuṇā — first through third jhāna.

## Part 4 — Equanimity (*Upekkhā*)

### §§ 140–175 — Upekkhā: even-minded toward all beings

**Equanimity** in the brahmavihāra sense is not indifference (*aññāṇupekkhā*) — the equanimity of not caring. It is *tatramajjhattatā* — the quality of standing in the middle, unmoved by partiality, neither drawn toward the pleasant nor pushed from the unpleasant. It is the mind's natural equilibrium when it fully recognizes the law of kamma: beings fare according to their own actions; my anxiety or grief cannot alter that.

**Starting point**: begin with a neutral person, then extend to the beloved, then to the disliked, then to all beings. Unlike mettā (which begins with the loved), upekkhā begins with the neutral because it is *already* equanimous toward neutral persons — the meditator simply recognizes that equanimity and then extends it.

**Near enemy**: *aññāṇupekkhā* — the pseudo-equanimity of not caring, of emotional dullness, of unfeeling detachment. This is the ever-present risk in upekkhā practice. The test: does the meditator still *see* the suffering of beings and respond wisely, or has the practice produced numbness?

**Far enemy**: the far enemy of upekkhā is the greed-and-aversion (*rāga-dosa*) that takes sides.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on developing upekkhā and its near enemy (§§ 140–165)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

### Why upekkhā produces the fourth jhāna

Upekkhā brahmavihāra reaches the **fourth jhāna** — the only brahmavihāra to do so. The reason is structural: the fourth jhāna is defined by the purity of equanimity and mindfulness (*upekkhāsatipārisuddhi*). The brahmavihāra of upekkhā has exactly the same quality — unmoved, clear, neither attracted nor repelled. When the meditator develops upekkhā toward all beings with the same steady equanimity that the fourth jhāna brings to all objects, the two merge.

In contrast, mettā, karuṇā, and muditā all carry a quality of *sukha* — the happiness of well-wishing — which is present in jhānas one through three but transcended in the fourth.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on upekkhā and the fourth jhāna (§§ 165–175)

## Summary: Brahmavihāras and the Jhāna Map

| Brahmavihāra | Pali | Near enemy | Jhāna range | Defining quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loving-kindness | *Mettā* | Worldly affection (*pema*) | J1–J3 | *Sukha*: the wish that beings be happy |
| Compassion | *Karuṇā* | Worldly grief (*domanassa*) | J1–J3 | *Sukha*: the care that beings be free from pain |
| Sympathetic joy | *Muditā* | Exuberance (*pahāsa*) | J1–J3 | *Sukha*: delight in beings' happiness |
| Equanimity | *Upekkhā* | Unfeeling detachment (*aññāṇupekkhā*) | J4 | *Upekkhā*: even-minded toward all |

|---|---|
