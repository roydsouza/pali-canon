## Overview

Chapter X stands at the summit of the Visuddhimagga's concentration section. The four **formless attainments** (*arūpa-samāpatti*) or *arūpa-jhānas* are the most refined states of concentration available within the meditation system of the rūpa-jhānas. They are not, strictly speaking, four additional jhānas alongside the four material jhānas — they are a different register of attainment, built on the fourth rūpa-jhāna as their indispensable foundation.

The canonical texts call all eight attainments (four rūpa-jhānas + four arūpa-jhānas) the **eight liberations** (*aṭṭha vimokkha*) and the **eight attainments** (*aṭṭha samāpatti*). Chapter XIII (not treated in this vault) covers the further mastery of all eight as a unified system.

---

## The Foundation: Fourth Rūpa-Jhāna as the Entry-Point

The four formless attainments cannot be entered from any of the first three rūpa-jhānas. They require the **fourth rūpa-jhāna** as their direct basis. The reason: the fourth jhāna is characterized by the complete transcendence of *sukha* and *dukkha* and the purity of equanimity and mindfulness. It is from this perfectly still, equanimous ground that the mind can "step out" of the materiality dimension altogether.

The transition to the first formless attainment is described as: take the kasiṇa (or any form object used in the fourth jhāna), and direct the mind to *transcend the perception of form entirely* — not by suppressing it but by attending to the space that was occupied by the form. This is the entry to the first arūpa-jhāna.

## The Four Formless Attainments

### §§ 16–50 — First: Base of Infinite Space (*Ākāsānañcāyatana*)

**Entry method**: emerge from the fourth rūpa-jhāna. Mentally "remove" or transcend the kasiṇa (the form object). Attend to the **space** that the kasiṇa occupied — infinite, unbounded in all directions. Hold this perception: *infinite space, infinite space* (*ananto ākāso*).

**Phenomenology**: the meditator is no longer attending to any form whatsoever — no kasiṇa color, no conceptual shape, no body sensation. The object is pure space. This is the first step beyond the rūpa realm.

**The jhāna factors**: the two factors of the fourth rūpa-jhāna (equanimity and one-pointedness) persist, but now directed to infinite space rather than a material form. The quality of equanimity is unchanged; only the object has shifted.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on infinite space (§§ 16–35)

### §§ 51–90 — Second: Base of Infinite Consciousness (*Viññāṇañcāyatana*)

**Entry method**: emerge from the base of infinite space. Do not attend to infinite space. Instead, attend to the **consciousness** that knew infinite space — which is itself infinite, since it knew something infinite. Hold this perception: *infinite consciousness, infinite consciousness* (*anantaṃ viññāṇaṃ*).

This shift is subtle: the first arūpa-jhāna took unbounded space as its object; the second takes the mind that knew that space as its object. The mind turns back on itself, attending to consciousness rather than to any external or spatial object.

**The structural move**: the entire sequence of formless attainments follows this pattern — each successive attainment takes the *preceding attainment* as its object (or the absence of the preceding) and transcends it. The mind peels away successive layers of perceptual content.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on infinite consciousness (§§ 51–70)

### §§ 91–130 — Third: Base of Nothingness (*Ākiñcaññāyatana*)

**Entry method**: emerge from the base of infinite consciousness. Do not attend to infinite consciousness. Instead, attend to the **absence of that consciousness** — the nothingness (*kiñci natthi*) where the infinite consciousness was. Hold this perception: *there is nothing, there is nothing* (*natthi kiñci*).

This is the most difficult transition in the sequence. The meditator is attending neither to space nor to consciousness but to the sheer *absence* of anything — a thoroughgoing nothingness that is itself the object of a very refined attention. The quality is described as serene, utterly still, with even the subtle movement of consciousness-attending-to-itself gone.

**Phenomenology**: practitioners describe the third arūpa as having a quality of profound peace and withdrawal. There is no "thing" present at all in the field of awareness; what persists is simply awareness of absence. The perception is *natthi kiñci* — "there is nothing whatsoever."

### §§ 131–175 — Fourth: Base of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception (*Nevasaññānāsaññāyatana*)

**Entry method**: emerge from the base of nothingness. Do not attend to nothingness. Instead, attend to the base of nothingness itself as "**neither gross nor absent**" — perceiving it as "peaceful, peaceful" (*santam etaṃ paṇītam etaṃ*). The name reflects its paradoxical character: perception (*saññā*) is present but so subtle that it cannot be called gross perception; yet absence of perception (*a-saññā*) is not the right description either.

**Structural position**: this is the most refined attainment accessible within the samādhi framework without insight. It is as close to the cessation of all mental activity as the mind can get while still functioning.

The canonical description (DN 9): the Venerable Citta asks whether the formless attainments are *saññā* or not. The Buddha's answer is that they are a form of perception, but extremely subtle — hence "neither-perception-nor-non-perception."

**Why not the fifth**: Buddhaghosa explains that there is no fifth arūpa-jhāna within the samādhi framework. The next step — *nirodha-samāpatti* (cessation of perception and feeling) — is not a concentration attainment at all. It is only accessible to *anāgāmis* and *arahants* who also have the four rūpa-jhānas and all four arūpa-jhānas, and who enter it with insight rather than samādhi as the driving force.

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on neither-perception-nor-non-perception (§§ 131–160)

## The Eight Attainments as a Unified System

| # | Attainment | Object | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First rūpa-jhāna | Meditation subject (e.g. breath nimitta) | — |
| 2 | Second rūpa-jhāna | Same | First jhāna |
| 3 | Third rūpa-jhāna | Same | Second jhāna |
| 4 | Fourth rūpa-jhāna | Same | Third jhāna |
| 5 | Base of infinite space | Infinite space (transcending form) | Fourth rūpa-jhāna |
| 6 | Base of infinite consciousness | Consciousness that knew infinite space | Base of infinite space |
| 7 | Base of nothingness | Absence of that consciousness | Base of infinite consciousness |
| 8 | Neither-perception-nor-non-perception | The base of nothingness itself as subtle | Base of nothingness |

## Cessation of Perception and Feeling (*Nirodha-Samāpatti*)

Beyond the eight attainments lies **nirodha-samāpatti** — the temporary cessation of all perception and feeling. Buddhaghosa treats this in Chapter XIII (not in this vault). Key points for context:

- It is **not a ninth jhāna** in the standard sense; it is the culmination of a path that combines *both* samatha (through all eight attainments) *and* insight (at least the level of non-return).
- It is accessible only to **anāgāmis and arahants** who have mastered all eight attainments.
- It is entered by proceeding through the eight attainments in order, then resolving to cease perception and feeling for a pre-determined period (up to seven days).
- During nirodha-samāpatti, the body remains sitting but all mental activity has stopped. Monks in ancient India were sometimes checked to see if they had entered this state rather than having died.

## Formless Attainments and Insight

A critical doctrinal question: do the formless attainments produce liberation? Buddhaghosa's answer, consistent with the canonical position: **no, not by themselves.** A meditator who masters all eight attainments but has no insight (*vipassanā*) into the three characteristics (*tilakkhaṇa*) will be reborn in the arūpa brahma realms and — after an extraordinarily long lifespan — will still face further existence.

The formless attainments can be turned into a basis for insight by emerging from them and then observing the arising and passing of the mental factors that constituted them. In this way, samatha serves as the vehicle for vipassanā, rather than an end in itself.

The fourth rūpa-jhāna, however, is the more commonly recommended basis for insight in the Theravāda tradition — it combines the equanimity and clarity needed for observation without the risk of being too absorbed in the subtle perception of the arūpa realms.
