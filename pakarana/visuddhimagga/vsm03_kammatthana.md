## Overview

Chapter III stands at the gateway of the Visuddhimagga's samādhi section. Before Buddhaghosa treats any individual meditation subject, he addresses three foundational questions: *Who should guide the meditator's choice of subject? What are the available subjects? And which subject suits which person?* The chapter's answer has shaped Theravāda meditation instruction for fifteen centuries.

---

## §§ 1–28 — The Kalyāṇamitta: Finding the Right Teacher

### The indispensable condition

Buddhaghosa opens with a statement that is, in a sense, the most important in the entire Visuddhimagga: before taking up any meditation subject, a meditator must find a **kalyāṇamitta** (*good friend*, spiritual friend). This is not optional counsel. The kalyāṇamitta's role is to assess the student's temperament and match the practice accordingly — an error here will waste years.

The canonical basis: the Buddha's famous exchange with Ānanda, who said that good friendship is "half the holy life." The Buddha corrected him: "Not so, Ānanda. Good friendship is the *whole* of the holy life."

> [!QUOTE]- Bhikkhu Bodhi — on the kalyāṇamitta (§§ 1–10)
> *[Paste relevant passage from your copy of The Path of Purification here]*

### What to look for in a teacher

Buddhaghosa lists the qualities of a capable teacher of meditation: virtue, learning, personal meditative experience with the subjects taught, ability to read and respond to a student's temperament, patience, willingness to explain, and genuine care. The most important is personal attainment — the teacher must know what they are describing from the inside.

Where a fully qualified teacher is unavailable, the meditator may turn to texts (the canonical suttas, the Visuddhimagga itself) as a secondary guide. But this is understood as a concession, not an equivalent.

## §§ 29–110 — The Forty Meditation Subjects (*Kammaṭṭhāna*)

Buddhaghosa enumerates 40 meditation subjects, organized into seven categories. This is the most comprehensive catalogue of formal meditation objects in classical Pali literature.

| Category | Count | Subjects |
|---|---|---|
| **Kasiṇas** (*kasiṇa*) | 10 | Earth, water, fire, wind; blue, yellow, red, white; light, enclosed space |
| **Foulness** (*asubha*) | 10 | Corpse stages: bloated, livid, festering, cut-up, gnawed, scattered, hacked-and-scattered, bleeding, worm-infested, skeleton |
| **Recollections** (*anussati*) | 10 | Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha, virtue, generosity, devas; death; body; breathing; peace |
| **Divine abidings** (*brahmavihāra*) | 4 | Loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity |
| **Formless states** (*arūpa*) | 4 | Infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, neither-perception-nor-non-perception |
| **Repulsiveness of food** | 1 | *Āhāre paṭikūlasaññā* |
| **Analysis of elements** | 1 | *Catudhātuvavatthāna* (four-element contemplation) |

**Total: 40.**

Not all subjects lead to full jhāna. Of the 40:
- **All four jhānas**: 10 kasiṇas; ānāpānasati; kāyagatāsati (entering the fourth jhāna via earth kasiṇa embedded in body-contemplation)
- **First three jhānas only**: mettā, karuṇā, muditā (because they are associated with *sukha*, which is absent in the fourth)
- **Fourth jhāna only**: upekkhā brahmavihāra
- **Formless jhānas**: the four arūpa subjects (built on the fourth rūpa-jhāna as foundation)
- **Access concentration only**: 6 recollections (Buddha through devas), maraṇasati, āhāre paṭikūlasaññā, catudhātuvavatthāna
- **Foulness meditations**: first jhāna only (vitakka and vicāra remain active on the repulsive object)

## §§ 74–100 — Temperament Types (*Carita*) and Subject Matching

This section is the practical heart of Chapter III. Buddhaghosa identifies **six temperament types** and matches meditation subjects to each. The match is not meant as permanent assignment; it reflects the object that will most quickly still the predominant mental tendency.

| Temperament | Pali | Predominant quality | Recommended subjects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lustful | *Rāga-carita* | Craving, attachment, beauty-seeking | Foulness meditations (asubha); kāyagatāsati |
| Hateful | *Dosa-carita* | Aversion, hardness, irritability | Four brahmavihāras; color kasiṇas (which evoke pleasure) |
| Deluded | *Moha-carita* | Dullness, confusion, scattered attention | Ānāpānasati (provides a clear, simple object that reveals the mind's movement) |
| Faithful | *Saddhā-carita* | Strong conviction, devotion | First six recollections (Buddhānussati through Devatānussati) |
| Intellectual | *Buddhi-carita* | Conceptual analysis, philosophical tendency | Maraṇasati; food repulsiveness; four-element analysis |
| Speculative | *Vitakka-carita* | Proliferating, restless discursive thought | Ānāpānasati |

The kasiṇa subjects (particularly earth and the light kasiṇa) and ānāpānasati are described as suitable for all temperament types, especially when the temperament is mixed or unclear.

## §§ 101–125 — Procedure for Taking Up the Subject

Once the teacher has identified the appropriate subject, the student goes alone to a **secluded dwelling** (*senāsana*) — ideally a forest, the foot of a tree, a cave, or an empty building — where the five cords of sensual pleasure are absent and sounds that disturb concentration are infrequent.

Buddhaghosa gives detailed environmental guidance: ideal and non-ideal monastery types, distance from villages, the importance of a settled community, and how to handle practical obstacles. The instruction reflects a milieu of full-time monastics; the lay practitioner adapts the principle: **find the least-disturbed environment available for the practice period.**

The chapter closes with the prescription to **take up the subject at the teacher's feet** — receive it in person, not from a book alone — and then go immediately to the secluded place and apply it, returning to the teacher for correction as needed.

## Key Pali terms in this chapter

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **kammaṭṭhāna** | Meditation subject; literally "working ground" — the object on which the meditator works |
| **kalyāṇamitta** | Good friend; spiritual companion; here, the teacher who assigns the meditation subject |
| **carita** | Temperament; the predominant mental tendency that shapes which subject is most effective |
| **kasiṇa** | Totality device; a physical disk or natural element used as the first object of concentration |
| **asubha** | Foulness; contemplation of the unattractive aspects of the body, classically via corpse stages |
| **anussati** | Recollection; mindful calling to mind of a spiritually significant object |
| **brahmavihāra** | Divine abiding; the four boundless qualities (mettā, karuṇā, muditā, upekkhā) |
| **arūpa** | Formless; the four attainments beyond material-jhāna that use space, consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception as their objects |
| **āhāre paṭikūlasaññā** | Perception of repulsiveness in food; contemplating the origin, preparation, and dissolution of what one eats |
| **catudhātuvavatthāna** | Analysis of the four elements; attending to the earth, water, fire, and air qualities in one's own body |
| **senāsana** | Dwelling; the physical place of practice — ideally secluded, quiet, and free of sensual distraction |
