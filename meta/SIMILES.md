# Simile Index (Upamā-saṅgaha)
**Navigation**: [[Texts-Index|Pali Canon Vault]]

This index compiles the most memorable similes (*upamā*) from the Pali Canon. The Buddha was famous for teaching through vivid comparison — these images are designed to be dwelled on, not just read.

---

## 🌊 Practice & Path

### 1. The Raft Simile (*Kollūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/majjhima_nikaya/mn22|MN 22: Alagaddūpamasutta]]
*   **Description**: A traveler builds a raft of grass, twigs, and branches to cross a great river. Having crossed, they do not carry the raft on their head; they leave it behind.
*   **Meaning**: The Dhamma is for crossing over (*nittharaṇattha*), not for holding onto (*gahaṇattha*). Even wholesome states must eventually be let go, let alone unwholesome ones.

### 2. The Lute Strings (*Vīṇūpama*)
*   **Source**: AN 6.55: Soṇasutta
*   **Description**: A lute cannot play a beautiful tune if the strings are stretched too tight or left too loose. It only plays well when tuned in balance.
*   **Meaning**: Energy (*viriya*) must be balanced with calmness. Too much energy leads to restlessness; too little leads to laziness.

### 3. The Six Animals (*Chappāṇaka*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn35|SN 35.247: Chappāṇakasutta]]
*   **Description**: Six different animals (dog, jackal, crocodile, bird, hyena, monkey) are tied to a single post with ropes. Each tries to pull towards its own habitat. They only rest when they are exhausted, or when the post is firm.
*   **Meaning**: The six sense bases pull the mind toward their objects. Mindfulness of the body (*kāyagatāsati*) is the firm post (*thambha*) that anchors the senses and prevents them from wandering.

### 4. The Chariot (*Rathavinītūpama*)
*   **Source**: MN 24: Rathavinītasutta (not yet migrated)
*   **Description**: Seven relay chariots ferry a traveler from Sāvatthī to Sāket. No single chariot makes the entire journey; each is exchanged for the next.
*   **Meaning**: Venerable Puṇṇa explains the seven purifications (*satta visuddhi*) to Sāriputta — each stage of the path is the vehicle for the next, culminating in final Nibbāna without clinging.
*   **See also**: [[matika/seven_purifications|Mātikā: Seven Purifications]]

### 5. The Rāhula's Mirror (*Ādāsūpama*)
*   **Source**: MN 61: Ambalaṭṭhikārāhulovādasutta (not yet migrated)
*   **Description**: A mirror is for the purpose of reflection; so too, actions, speech, and thoughts should be repeatedly reflected upon before, during, and after doing them.
*   **Meaning**: Moral training (*sīla*) is grounded in self-reflection. Before acting, ask: "Will this harm me or others?" After acting, review honestly.

### 6. The Saw (*Kakacūpama*)
*   **Source**: MN 21: Kakacūpamasutta (not yet migrated)
*   **Description**: Even if bandits were to cut you limb by limb with a two-handled saw, if any of you were to harbour ill will towards them you would not be following my teaching.
*   **Meaning**: The highest standard of patient endurance. Loving-kindness (*mettā*) must remain unbroken even in the most extreme circumstances.
*   **Practice**: Recite as a contemplation on mettā when anger arises.
*   **See also**: [[matika/ill_will_or_hostility|Mātikā: Ill Will]]

### 7. The Log in the River (*Dārukkhandūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn35|SN 35.241: Dārukkhandopama-sutta]]
*   **Description**: A log floating in the river will reach the sea if it does not get stuck on the near bank, the far bank, or in the middle; if it is not seized by a person, rotted from within, or stranded on an island.
*   **Meaning**: The practitioner of the spiritual path will reach Nibbāna if they avoid being held by near-side defilements (own-body identity), far-side defilements (views about future), or stopped by the middle (sensual pleasure). Rottenness from within = defilement. Stranded on an island = delightful states of being.

### 8. The Flood-Crosser (*Oghaviṭṭha*)
*   **Source**: SN 1.1: Oghavagga (SN 1 not yet migrated)
*   **Description**: A deva asks how the Buddha crossed the flood (*ogha*). "By not stopping and by not straining, friend, I crossed the flood."
*   **Meaning**: The middle way applied to practice itself: not resting in complacency (*ogilāyamāno*) nor forcing effort (*āyūhāyana*). Moving forward lightly and continuously.

## 🧩 The Five Aggregates

### 1. The Five Natural Metaphors (*Phenapiṇḍūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn22|SN 22.95: Phenapiṇḍūpamasutta]]
*   **Description**:
    *   **Material Form (Rūpa)** is compared to a **lump of foam** floating down the Ganges.
    *   **Feeling (Vedanā)** is compared to a **water bubble** in rain.
    *   **Perception (Saññā)** is compared to a **mirage** in the heat of midday.
    *   **Volitional Formations (Saṅkhārā)** are compared to a **plantain trunk** (which has no heartwood).
    *   **Consciousness (Viññāṇa)** is compared to a **conjurer's trick/illusion**.
*   **Meaning**: Illustrates the emptiness (*suññatā*), insubstantiality, and lack of core self in the five aggregates.

### 2. The Sheaves of Reeds (*Naḷakalāpī*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn12|SN 12.67: Naḷakalāpīsutta]]
*   **Description**: Two sheaves of reeds lean against each other. If one sheave is pulled away, the other falls down.
*   **Meaning**: Illustrates reciprocal conditioning in dependent origination. Consciousness (*viññāṇa*) and name-and-form (*nāmarūpa*) condition each other; one cannot exist without the other in a living being.

### 3. The Elephant's Footprint (*Hatthipadūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/majjhima_nikaya/mn28|MN 28: Mahāhatthipadopamasutta]]
*   **Description**: The footprint of any walking animal easily fits inside the massive footprint of an elephant.
*   **Meaning**: All wholesome qualities, including the five aggregates, fit within the framework of the Four Noble Truths. As the elephant's footprint is the largest, the Four Noble Truths encompass all of the Buddha's teaching.

### 4. The Burning House (*Ādittapariyāya*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn35|SN 35.28: Ādittapariyāyasutta]] (also attributed to the Fire Sermon)
*   **Description**: All six sense bases and their objects, contacts, feelings, and cravings are burning — burning with the fires of passion, aversion, and delusion.
*   **Meaning**: The entire sensory world, when experienced without mindfulness, is a burning house. The practice is to understand the burning and find the cool refuge of Nibbāna.

## 🎯 Mind & Defilements

### 1. The Poisoned Arrow (*Visalyūpama*)
*   **Source**: MN 63: Cūḷamāluṅkyasutta (not yet migrated)
*   **Description**: A man struck by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows the archer's name, caste, height, complexion, the type of bow, and the type of arrow. He dies before any of these questions can be answered.
*   **Meaning**: Metaphysical questions — is the cosmos eternal? Does the Tathāgata exist after death? — do not help with the arrow of suffering already lodged in the mind. The practice is to remove the arrow now.
*   **See also**: [[matika/suffering|Mātikā: Dukkha]]

### 2. The Snake-Handler (*Alagaddūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/majjhima_nikaya/mn22|MN 22: Alagaddūpamasutta]]
*   **Description**: A man who wants to catch a snake grabs it by the body or tail. It turns and bites him. A skilled snake-handler grips it behind the head with a forked stick and it cannot bite.
*   **Meaning**: The Dhamma seized wrongly — for argument, debate, or self-justification — turns and bites. The Dhamma grasped skillfully, by its purpose (to abandon defilements), is harmless and beneficial.

### 3. The Blind Men Assessing the Elephant (*Gajāndha*)
*   **Source**: Udāna 6.4 (variant in AN; popular tradition)
*   **Description**: Several blind men each touch a different part of an elephant and assert their partial experience is the whole truth — the ear is a fan, the leg a pillar, the tail a rope.
*   **Meaning**: Partial views of reality lead to contention. Those who have not "seen" the Dhamma directly mistake their limited experience for the whole. Used to illustrate sectarian dispute.

### 4. The Peg in the Tree (*Rukkha-sūpama*)
*   **Source**: AN 3.101
*   **Description**: A tree struck by fire from an ember carried by the wind can be extinguished. But a tree struck from within — rotted by a fungus — is consumed completely.
*   **Meaning**: The defilement of a person who is attacked from outside (by others, circumstances) can be overcome. But the defilement that arises from within — lust, hatred, delusion that have not been abandoned — destroys from the inside.

## 🏆 Awakening & Freedom

### 1. Vegetables Boiled Dry in a Pot (*Sukkhaḍāka*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/khuddaka_nikaya/therigatha/thig1_1|THIG 1.1: Aññatarātherīgāthā]]
*   **Description**: Leafy vegetables cooked in a pot with water are boiled dry until all the water is gone and no steam remains.
*   **Meaning**: Represents the complete eradication of desire and latent defilements through the fire of wisdom, leaving no trace for future rebirth.

### 2. The Blind Turtle (*Kāṇa-kacchapūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn56|SN 56.48: Chiggalayuga-sutta]]
*   **Description**: A blind turtle living in the great ocean surfaces once every hundred years. Somewhere on the ocean is a yoke with a single hole. The chance of the turtle surfacing through that hole is greater than the chance of a being in the lower realms gaining a human birth.
*   **Meaning**: Human birth is extraordinarily rare. Encountering the Dhamma in a human birth is rarer still. This moment is a gift. Urgency (*saṃvega*) is the appropriate response.

### 3. The Island in a Flood (*Dīpūpama*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/samyutta_nikaya/sn22|SN 22.43: Attadīpa-sutta]]
*   **Description**: The Buddha tells Ānanda to be an island to oneself, a refuge to oneself, with no other refuge; to be like an island.
*   **Meaning**: Final refuge is not an external teacher or deity but the practice itself — the four foundations of mindfulness. The Buddha, close to death, points inward.
*   **See also**: [[matika/four_foundations_of_mindfulness|Mātikā: Four Foundations of Mindfulness]]

### 4. The Lamp (*Dīpūpama — Liberation*)
*   **Source**: [[mula/sutta/khuddaka_nikaya/udana|Udāna 8.9: Nibbāna Sutta]]
*   **Description**: Just as a lamp goes out when its oil and wick are exhausted, the arahant at parinibbāna is like a flame that has simply gone out — not going anywhere, not remaining here.
*   **Meaning**: Nibbāna defies description as "existence" or "non-existence." The going-out of the flame is neither annihilation nor perpetuation — it is simply the ending of conditions.
*   **See also**: [[matika/cessation_of_suffering|Mātikā: Nirodha]]
